This is a traditional, heavy-duty aerospace construction method used for decades in aircraft like the Piper J-3 Cub, Taylorcraft, and many modern bush planes and kit aircraft (such as certain tube-and-fabric designs).

- **How it’s used:** Thin-walled structural steel tubing (almost always **4130 chrome-moly**) is cut, mitered to fit precisely against neighboring tubes, and **welded together at intersecting nodes (called "clusters")**. This creates a rigid, unified three-dimensional space-frame cage—most commonly used for the cabin safety cell, engine mounts, and landing gear attachments.
    
- **Why manufacturers use it:**
    
    - **Structural Strength and Rigidity:** Welded steel creates an incredibly strong, rigid structure that handles high G-forces and localized stress points exceptionally well.
        
    - **Crashworthiness:** Steel tubing is ductile and absorbs massive amounts of energy by bending and deforming progressively during a heavy impact, forming a protective safety cage around the occupants.
        
- **Engineering Trade-offs:**
    
    - **Weight:** Steel is much denser than aluminum. While chrome-moly allows walls to be made paper-thin (sometimes 0.035" or 0.049"), a full steel cage is generally heavier than an equivalent aluminum or composite structure.
        
    - **Corrosion Vulnerability:** Steel rusts. Because these tubes are hollow, moisture can condense inside them over time. Builders must coat the inside of the tubes with rust inhibitors (like Linseed oil or Internal Corrosion Preventive compounds) during assembly.
        
    - **Repair Complexity:** You cannot easily unbolt a damaged steel cluster in the field; fixing a cracked weld or bent tube requires a certified A&P mechanic skilled in gas or TIG welding.
