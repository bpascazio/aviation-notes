4130 is a low-alloy steel designated by the American Iron and Steel Institute (AISI) and Society of Automotive Engineers (SAE). The "41" indicates a alloy steel containing chromium and molybdenum, and the "30" indicates a nominal carbon content of roughly 0.30%.
### Why 4130 is the Industry Standard

Aircraft designers and manufacturers rely on 4130 tubing because it offers a exceptional balance of mechanical properties:

- **High Strength-to-Weight Ratio:** It is significantly stronger than standard carbon steel, allowing manufacturers to use thin-walled tubing (such as 0.035-inch or 0.049-inch walls) to keep the overall aircraft weight low.
    
- **Excellent Weldability:** It can be readily welded using TIG (GTAW) or oxy-acetylene welding techniques without losing structural integrity, provided proper pre-heating and cooling procedures are followed.
    
- **Ductility and Toughness:** It can absorb high shock loads and stresses (crucial for landing gear and airframes experiencing turbulence or rough field operations) without abruptly shattering.

While 4130 is the modern standard, you may encounter other materials depending on the aircraft's vintage or design:

- **1025 Carbon Steel:** Common in older, vintage aircraft (like early Piper Cubs). It has a lower tensile strength than 4130 and requires thicker walls to achieve comparable strength, making structures slightly heavier.
    
- **Stainless Steel:** Occasionally used in specific areas exposed to high heat or severe corrosive environments (like exhaust shrouds or firewall components), though rarely for primary structural tubing due to cost and fabrication challenges.
